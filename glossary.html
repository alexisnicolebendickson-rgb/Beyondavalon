Thesis terms, in plain language

Thesis Glossary

Three terms carry the argument of this project. Each one names something the research kept finding in the record — and each one is explained here the way the project means it, with one concrete Avalon example.

How to read these. These are working definitions from the thesis — Institutional Erasure, Mnemonic Hegemony, and the Avalon Plantation: A Genealogical Counter-Archive of the University of Alabama in Huntsville — translated out of academic language. The examples are real findings from this site's research.

Archival violence

The harm done not just by what's missing from the archive, but by how the archive was built. Enslaved people appear in this record with precision only when precision served a white property claim — a will inventories them like acreage; a court, sentencing a man to death, simultaneously set a price for what his death would cost his owner. And twentieth-century archives compounded it by filing Black history under white family surnames, so the people the records actually describe are submerged under the name of the family that enslaved them. Even the name "Avalon" is an example: a retrospective label with no primary-source authority, applied long after the fact, severing the site from its documented legal and genealogical history.

Avalon example: the Jones family papers, 1784–1940, are described in a multi-decade finding aid organized around the white Jones lineage. The collection's own time span suggests it also documents enslaved and later free Black Jones households — but their identities remain buried under the family surname that enslaved them. Until collections are re-indexed to foreground Black actors, the archive keeps doing the violence it was built to do.

Mnemonic hegemony

Whoever controls the story of a place decides what counts as its history. It is how institutions and public narratives establish what gets remembered, named, and made visible — and what quietly doesn't. It isn't always a lie. Often it's a cleaner, institution-favoring account built from the records the institution chose to keep and publish.

Avalon example: UAH's newsroom can publish "the documents" as the authoritative timeline of the Willie and Lola Jones land dispute on Athens Pike — precisely because the university holds the documents. The family's account — a forged 1958 signature, and closer to 10–12 acres taken rather than 2.25 — has no equivalent institutional platform. One side has a newsroom; the other has family memory. That imbalance is mnemonic hegemony operating in real time. See What UAH & Huntsville Knew for the full evidence.

Institutional erasure

Not forgetting by accident — forgetting as a system. Legal, archival, economic, and commemorative systems worked together so that white ownership was always explicit while Black names, kinship, property, labor, and burial were fragmented, contested, or administratively obscured. The argument is not that every omission came from one coordinated intention; it is that the mechanisms kept producing the same unequal visibility.

Avalon example: the Willie and Lola Jones homestead on Athens Pike. A 1954 condemnation over the family well. A 1958 warranty deed to a realty company headed by the City of Huntsville's own land-acquisition chief — a signature the family says was forged. A 1962 condemnation compensating Jones heirs and the Board of Trustees for fractional interests in the same tract, years before UAH was chartered. Land and paperwork moved cleanly through institutions and the people who controlled the filing; the family was left to reconstruct its own history from memory, testimony, and fragments. The university was founded on the land in 1969 with little public recognition of any of it.

These definitions will be refined as the thesis develops. If a term's use on this site drifts from the thesis, that counts as a correction owed — say so.
