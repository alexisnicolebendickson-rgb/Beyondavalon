Thesis terms, in plain language

Thesis Glossary

The first three terms carry the argument of this project. The rest are the working vocabulary of the research itself — the legal instruments, court processes, and methods you will meet in the Documents and Names Registry. Each one is explained here the way the thesis uses it, with one concrete Avalon example.

How to read these. These are working definitions from the thesis — Institutional Erasure, Mnemonic Hegemony, and the Avalon Plantation: A Genealogical Counter-Archive of the University of Alabama in Huntsville — translated out of academic language. The examples are real findings from this site's research.

Archival violence

The harm done not just by what's missing from the archive, but by how the archive was built. Enslaved people appear in this record with precision only when precision served a white property claim — a will inventories them like acreage; a court, sentencing a man to death, simultaneously set a price for what his death would cost his owner. And twentieth-century archives compounded it by filing Black history under white family surnames, so the people the records actually describe are submerged under the name of the family that enslaved them. Even the name "Avalon" is an example: a retrospective label with no primary-source authority, applied long after the fact, severing the site from its documented legal and genealogical history.

Avalon example: the Jones family papers, 1784–1940, are described in a multi-decade finding aid organized around the white Jones lineage. The collection's own time span suggests it also documents enslaved and later free Black Jones households — but their identities remain buried under the family surname that enslaved them. Until collections are re-indexed to foreground Black actors, the archive keeps doing the violence it was built to do.

Mnemonic hegemony

Whoever controls the story of a place decides what counts as its history. It is how institutions and public narratives establish what gets remembered, named, and made visible — and what quietly doesn't. It isn't always a lie. Often it's a cleaner, institution-favoring account built from the records the institution chose to keep and publish.

Avalon example: UAH's newsroom can publish "the documents" as the authoritative timeline of the Willie and Lola Jones land dispute on Athens Pike — precisely because the university holds the documents. The family's account — a forged 1958 signature, and closer to 10–12 acres taken rather than 2.25 — has no equivalent institutional platform. One side has a newsroom; the other has family memory. That imbalance is mnemonic hegemony operating in real time. See What UAH & Huntsville Knew for the full evidence.

Institutional erasure

Not forgetting by accident — forgetting as a system. Legal, archival, economic, and commemorative systems worked together so that white ownership was always explicit while Black names, kinship, property, labor, and burial were fragmented, contested, or administratively obscured. The argument is not that every omission came from one coordinated intention; it is that the mechanisms kept producing the same unequal visibility.

Avalon example: the Willie and Lola Jones homestead on Athens Pike. A 1954 condemnation over the family well. A 1958 warranty deed to a realty company headed by the City of Huntsville's own land-acquisition chief — a signature the family says was forged. A 1962 condemnation compensating Jones heirs and the Board of Trustees for fractional interests in the same tract, years before UAH was chartered. Land and paperwork moved cleanly through institutions and the people who controlled the filing; the family was left to reconstruct its own history from memory, testimony, and fragments. The university was founded on the land in 1969 with little public recognition of any of it.

More terms from the thesis

The three terms above carry the argument. The terms below are the working vocabulary of the research — drawn directly from the thesis (Institutional Erasure, Mnemonic Hegemony, and the Avalon Plantation) and the records it reads. Where the evidence is thin, the entry says so.

Manumission

The legal act of freeing an enslaved person — rare, tightly controlled, and never casual in antebellum Alabama.

Avalon example: Nelson Anderson's 1819 will, proved in 1820, contained an unusual act of manumission: an enslaved boy born in August 1815, Lindsay Bonapartte, whom Anderson “emancipated, and sett free, from shackles of slavery” — to take effect at Anderson's death, with a grandson appointed to see the boy educated and apprenticed to a trade.

The 1820 emancipation act

The legislative act that freed Elizabeth and her three children — Evelina, Ann, and Shandy. It is the documentary root of the Elizabeth's-descendants lineage traced on this site's Family & Findings page.

What it doesn't do: the act names only Elizabeth and her three children. It does not name Columbus Jones or Reuben Jones — so their connection to Elizabeth remains a genealogical question under investigation, not a fact the act establishes.

Counter-archive

Rebuilding the archive against its own grain. The thesis's method: systematically logging every Jones-related probate, census, chancery, and claims record under Black and mixed-heritage lineages rather than white surnames alone — the practice behind this site's Names Registry. It is in the thesis subtitle itself: A Genealogical Counter-Archive of the University of Alabama in Huntsville.

Chancery

The equity court that heard estate disputes, dower suits, and injunctions. Because federal census schedules omitted names, chancery records are often the only place an enslaved person's name survives — as a line item in an estate settlement. This project's Bedford County chancery research reconstructed Capt. John Jones's family, and the enslaved people attached to it, from exactly these files.

Probate

The court process of settling a dead person's estate: wills proved, inventories taken, heirs paid. Probate files — wills, estate papers, loose records — are where enslaved people were inventoried by name, which is why this project's database leans on them so heavily.

Dower

A widow's legal right to a share of her husband's estate. Widows like Frances B. Jones sued for it in chancery. And when a married couple sold land, the wife was examined privately, apart from her husband, and signed a dower release — sometimes the first time a woman's name appears anywhere on a deed.

Avalon example: Ruanner Jones signed the January 1834 deed with her mark and, after private examination apart from her husband, gave a full dower release — the first woman's name attached to Reuben P. Jones's deeds.

Deed of trust

A debt instrument pledging property — often enslaved people — as collateral for a loan.

Avalon examples: an 1842 deed of trust securing a $12,170.05 bank note named some fifty enslaved people; and the 1821 deed of trust at the center of this site's Dred Scott lead pledged eight enslaved people — including a man recorded as “Sam” and a boy named “Dred” — as collateral on a $2,000 Blow-family debt.

Warranty deed

An outright land sale in which the seller guarantees the title.

Avalon example: Reuben P. Jones and his wife Ruanner's January 1834 warranty deed conveying 36 acres to Levi Byram for $250 — Ruanner signed with her mark.

Condemnation

The government's taking of private land for public use, with compensation set through a legal process the landowner doesn't control. The thesis treats condemnation records not just as evidence of transfer but as instruments that decide whose relationship to land becomes publicly legible.

Avalon example: the 1954 condemnation of the Willie and Lola Jones family well, and the 1962 condemnation compensating Jones heirs for fractional interests in the homestead tract — the takings reconstructed document by document in this site's Argument section.

Hiring out

The practice of leasing an enslaved person's labor to someone else for a set term — one of the mechanisms of bondage this site covers on its dedicated page.

Avalon example: the January 1, 1844 hiring-out list placing roughly nineteen enslaved people for twelve months — Aaron to Joshua Derby at $81.50, and so on — each person named, priced, and placed.

Convict leasing

The post-emancipation system of leasing county convicts — overwhelmingly Black — to private contractors. Alabama's version fed the Birmingham-district mines and furnaces. The thesis flags the Alabama Department of Archives and History's 1881–1890 county-convict volumes as still-unsearched records that would name Madison County's prisoners, lessees, and destinations. See Slave & Convict Leasing for the full evidence.

Freedman's Bank

The Freedman's Savings and Trust Company, chartered for formerly enslaved people after the Civil War. Its Huntsville branch registers recorded each depositor's parentage, birthplace, occupation, and savings — what the thesis calls an “economic counter-archive” of Black attempts to build land-based wealth and financial security.

Avalon example: Columbus Jones's 1867 application and Reuben Jones's 1872 record (No. 1406) are both in the Huntsville registers.

Reconstruction-era legislator

Black men elected to Southern statehouses during Reconstruction. Shandy Wesley Jones represented Tuscaloosa County (1868–70); Columbus Jones represented Madison County (1868); Reuben Jones represented Madison County. The thesis treats them as largely forgotten Black legislators whose lives bridge plantation slavery and state politics.

FAN cluster

Friends, associates, neighbors: the people around an enslaver — bondsmen, will witnesses, executors, in-laws. Researching the cluster is a standard step in this project, because their records often name enslaved people the enslaver's own papers don't.

These definitions will be refined as the thesis develops. If a term's use on this site drifts from the thesis, that counts as a correction owed — say so.
