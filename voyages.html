The Middle Passage and the Landing Stops

The project's founding question was where the Jones family's wealth and power came from — and whether the transatlantic slave trade was part of the answer. This page reports what the documents actually show: the Bristol outfitting port, the crossings, the Caribbean landing stops, and — stated just as carefully — what has not been proven.

How this project treats the trade. Transatlantic and domestic/coastal findings are kept strictly separate: they are different trades, different archives, different evidence. A surname match is a lead, never an identification — “Jones” is one of the most common Anglophone surnames, and this page names the firewalls that keep look-alike Joneses apart.

Bristol: the outfitting port

Bristol was Britain's second slaving port, and the Jones name sits inside its merchant records. James Jones, merchant of Bristol — his will proved at the Prerogative Court of Canterbury (PROB 11/1259/104) after his death in 1795 — outfitted slave ships in the trade's final legal decade. His will, purchased from The National Archives in September 2026, is the documentary anchor for the Bristol end of this story.

The scholarly slave-trader register confirms the scale of the trade around him: it records a separate Thomas Jones moving roughly 20,000 captive Africans across 47 crossings between 1752 and 1796 — and states explicitly that this trader was not the Thomas Jones whom James Jones's will named as his brother, because James never invested alongside a Thomas Jones. The brother and the trader are two men. They stay separate.

A 2026 query of the Voyages transatlantic slave-trade database (2020 release) now quantifies his fleet: 62 voyages name Jones, James as owner between 1783 and 1794, sailing from Bristol and landing overwhelmingly in Jamaica, with roughly 13,900 recorded disembarkations across the 49 voyages that report numbers. Not one fleet voyage touches Virginia or the Carolinas: the wealth was made in the Jamaica trade. These figures are provisional — 28 of the 62 carry the dataset's own qualified attribution, and the live database has added voyages since 2020 — but this is the strongest candidate yet for the family's slave-trade capital base.

The Royal Charlotte’s crossings

James Jones's ship was the Royal Charlotte. Her captain was James Soutar, a Scottish-born mariner who had migrated to Bristol and was commanding slave ships by 1791. Soutar sailed the Royal Charlotte four times for Jones — three as master (1791–1792, 1792–1793, and 1794), plus an earlier 1790–1791 sailing as second captain under William Peale (voyage 18083: departed Bristol 21 October 1790, landed Jamaica 11 May 1791, 312 people disembarked) — and after Jones's death in 1795 he left Bristol for Liverpool, where he commanded five more slaving voyages between 1797 and 1804 (the Ellis, then the Frances). On the last of them, the Frances (1803–1804), Soutar sailed as part-owner and landed at Charleston, South Carolina — his only voyage touching the Carolinas. He died on 1 October 1819 possessed of just £200.

Four sailings in five years, in the trade's last legal decade: the Royal Charlotte is the one documented vessel tying a Jones directly to the Middle Passage.

Jamaica: the landing stop

The densest Jones footprint in the Caribbean records sits in Saint Catherine, Jamaica. Nine slave returns name an “Honorable James Jones” — eight in 1817 (T 71/1, pp. 655–662) and one on 28 June 1820 (T 71/2, p. 325) — with roughly 300 enslaved people named across them, alongside co-enslavers Francis Graham and George Guest. The honorific suggests a Jamaica assembly or council office, but the returns alone do not establish who he was: his identity is Unresolved, and he is not merged with anyone — not the Bristol trader, who died in 1795, and not the man below.

A second Jamaica figure is better documented: James Jones of Great Baddow, owner of Twickenham Park, St Catherine, from 1807 to 1834. His 30 January 1836 compensation claim covered 240 enslaved people and £4,282 15s 2d — awarded not to Jones but to the London merchants Samuel Baker and Thomas Phillpotts (the firm Baker Phillpotts) as mortgagees and judgment creditors, with Jones listed as beneficiary and remainderman. The mortgage trail runs straight from a Jamaica plantation to a London counting house — the metropolitan end of the wealth chain, and a cluster of associated names worth opening.

Barbados: one man, named

The Barbados record is smaller and more precise. The 1817 slave return of Major George Juxon (T 71/520, p. 326), verified against the manuscript image, records one enslaved man as his own property: Temple — male, Black, house servant, age 40, country listed as African. One line in a register, one life reduced to five data points, and the only thing this project can still do for him is keep his name exact.

The Virginia negative, stated plainly: a search of University College London's Legacies of British Slavery database finds no Jones slave-owners with Virginia links. That is structural, not evidential: Virginia was not a British slave colony at emancipation, so no compensation claims exist for it. The search closes the UCL–Virginia route and nothing more — it proves nothing about who did or didn't hold people in Virginia.

The firewalls: Joneses who are not each other

Because the surname is common and the records are full of look-alikes, this project keeps documented firewalls between them:

  • Thomas Jones the trader (~20,000 Africans, 47 crossings, d. October 1795) is not James Jones's brother Thomas — the register says so explicitly.
  • Richard Jones is two men: the Liverpool trader who died 18 December 1799 (7 crossings, including the Unicorn), and the Royal African Company subscriber who held £149 of RAC stock in 1726. Same name, different man, different century — do not merge.
  • Philip Jones, named as a presumed brother and partner of James Jones around 1784–94, has no register entry — Unresolved, and the missing entry is not evidence he did not exist.
  • The William-and-Jane baptism candidate is not merged with the slave trader.
  • Bristol's James Jones (d. 1795) is not Jamaica's “Honorable James Jones” — Unresolved either way.
  • Colonel Francis Jones of Bermuda — investor in 14 intra-American voyages, 1729–1745 (~140 embarked, ~125 disembarked, bought in Bermuda, Jamaica, and St Kitts; landed at Hampton, the James River, and New York) — resolves to a wealthy Paget Parish merchant and militia colonel, not a Virginian. Lead closed.

What connects to Madison County — and what doesn't

Here is the honest accounting. What is documented: a Bristol Jones outfitting slave ships in the 1790s; a named vessel and captain; a Jamaica footprint of roughly 300 enslaved people under another Jones's name; a mortgage chain from Twickenham Park to London merchants; and, in Madison County, an Avalon line whose documented wealth is enslaved-labor wealth (47 enslaved people in 1820 per the probate inventory, 139 in 1850, 96 in 1860), creditor and commercial wealth, and land speculation.

What is not documented: any record connecting James Jones of Bristol to Lewellen Jones's Madison County line. That connection is Unresolved — not disproven. The single biggest unrun test remains a full SlaveVoyages surname query for Jones as owner, investor, captain, and enslaver, in both the transatlantic and intra-American databases. Until it runs, absence of evidence is all this page claims.

Why this page exists. The thesis asks where the wealth and the power came from. The transatlantic trade is the hardest part of that question to answer honestly — the archives that would connect a Bristol counting house to a Madison County cotton field either never existed or were never kept. This page holds the documented pieces apart from the missing ones, so the missing ones stay visible as missing.
