Eminent Domain

The modern endpoint of a century of erasure: the mid-twentieth-century condemnation of the Willie Jones homestead on Athens Pike — now Holmes Avenue — on the land that became UAH's Business Administration Complex. This page carries the corrected chronology, with the family's account and the official record kept carefully apart.

How to read this page. Documented = stated in a primary or institutional source; Disputed = the family and the institutions give conflicting accounts; Unresolved = the public record does not settle it. The family's account and the official record are attributed separately — they are not blended.

The land and the claim

Investigative reporting between 2023 and 2025 documents that five surviving Jones siblings claimed land along Athens Pike — now Holmes Avenue — as having remained in their family since approximately 1870. The UAH Business Administration building currently occupies the site. Documented (as reported claims).

Those reports relay the family's understanding that Columbus Jones is their great-great-great-grandfather and Shandy Wesley Jones a great-great-great-uncle — a testable hypothesis that, if established through primary genealogical records, would directly connect Reconstruction-era Black political leadership to a mid-century land dispossession. It has not been established. Unresolved.

1954: the city condemns the well

In 1954, the City of Huntsville — not the county — condemned a 60-by-40-foot well-site parcel on the Jones family's land (Probate Cause No. 12257; Council Resolutions 54-109 and 54-127). The Probate Court's initial compensation award was $900 (December 30, 1954); a circuit-court jury raised it to $1,250 on appeal (November 7, 1955). Documented.

The condemnation landed inside a documented municipal water crisis: the city had handed its municipal water system to a new waterworks utility board because the supply was “polluted and inadequate,” and the well condemnation was authorized one month later. The official record's stated purpose is securing a clean public water supply. Documented.

“The water was declared unfit” is the family's and the news account — not the official record's language. No health or inspection order declaring the well unfit has been located. This page carries the family's account as the family's account, and the official record as the official record. Disputed rationale, attributed.

1958: the disputed deed

On October 15, 1958, a warranty deed conveyed two tracts, including a 2.25-acre parcel, to W. L. Sanderson Realty — a company headed by the City of Huntsville's own land-acquisition chief. Willie Jones's surviving children say their father was illiterate, always signed with an “X,” and that the signature on this deed was forged — an allegation supported by a handwriting analyst's opinion. Documented as an allegation; the forgery itself is disputed.

UAH states that Willie Jones and his immediate family, through legal counsel, were compensated when Madison County and the City of Huntsville acquired the parcel and subsequently conveyed it to the University's Board of Trustees — and that neither the Board nor UAH ever acquired the land directly from the family. The family disputes the acreage figure and the deed. Disputed.

The disputed acreage — reported as 2.25 acres by UAH and as 10 to 12 acres by family representatives — remains unresolved in the public record, as does the discrepancy between the reported deed dates of 1954 and 1958. Unresolved.

The chain this closes

The thesis traces one long chain of custody behind this parcel: from the 1825 University Fund — seeded in part by $3,698.16¼ that Alabama realized from the sale of Africans seized in violation of federal law, deposited to the state treasurer's credit — through antebellum trust deeds, through the Southern Claims Commission files, through the tenant-lien systems, to the mid-twentieth-century condemnation of the Willie Jones homestead. What is missing, the thesis is explicit about, is a comprehensive legal analysis connecting antebellum trust deeds directly to the modern property titles the university holds. That analysis is still needed. Under investigation.

Why this page exists. Eminent domain is where every thread of this project meets in the present tense: the archive that indexed Black families under white names, the credit systems that kept Black labor tethered, the court that priced a man's execution — and finally the city's condemnation docket, converting the last of the family's land into the ground under a business school. The paper calls it the modern endpoint of the erasure. The endpoint is still standing.

Drawn from the thesis section “20th-Century Culmination: Eminent Domain” (September 2026) and the project's evidence table. The full documentary record is on What UAH & Huntsville Knew; the burial ground beneath the campus is mapped on the UAH Campus Map.
