Direct evidence

What UAH & Huntsville Knew

This page collects, in one place, the documented record of what the University of Alabama in Huntsville, the UA System Board of Trustees, Madison County, and the City of Huntsville knew — and when — about the former Jones/Avalon plantation land beneath campus and the Willie and Lola Jones homestead on Athens Pike. Every row below is drawn from a named source. Where the record is contested, both sides are shown.

Why this page exists. "Institutional erasure" is easy to assert and hard to prove. This page exists to make the underlying evidence checkable: dates, documents, and direct quotations, laid out as a timeline and an evidence table rather than folded into narrative prose. This material also appears within The Argument section of the home page; it is repeated here as a stand-alone, citable reference. A note on naming: "Avalon" is a retrospective label (per a 1968–71 Harrison family history), not the plantation's documented period name.

Direct answer or finding

The record indicates that both UAH and the City of Huntsville/Madison County were direct parties to land transactions on the parcel now disputed by the Willie and Lola Jones family, and that UAH's own newsroom has published the deed and condemnation history for that parcel. The record also indicates the university has taken an official public position — that the Jones family was compensated and that neither the Board of Trustees nor UAH ever acquired the land directly from the family — while the family disputes the acreage figure and alleges the 1958 deed bearing Willie Jones's signature was forged. Additional documentation, specifically the complete original condemnation and payment file, is needed before the acreage and forgery questions can be resolved by an independent party rather than by either side's own account.

Interpretation

Read end to end, this timeline shows an institution that was a documented legal party to the land — receiving deeds, appearing in condemnation filings, and holding fractional interests years before its own 1969 founding — and that today controls the public narrative about how that land was acquired. That is not, by itself, evidence of wrongdoing; property does routinely change hands through condemnation, realty intermediaries, and heir settlements. What it does establish is that UAH and the Board of Trustees were positioned close enough to these transactions, early enough, that "the university had no direct dealings with the Jones family" is a narrower and more careful claim than "the university's predecessor entities and land-acquisition network had no knowledge of, or connection to, the disputed transfer." The 1966 topographic map and the undated Holmes Avenue engineering sheets matter here specifically because they show planning and infrastructure activity already underway over this exact corridor before or around the disputed period — contemporaneous documentation, not retrospective claim.

This case sits inside the same pattern this project documents at Avalon a century earlier: land and paperwork move cleanly through institutions and the parties who control filing and indexing, while the people losing land are left to reconstruct their own history from family memory, oral testimony, and whatever fragments of the record survive. Sociologically, this is Jeffrey Olick's mnemonic hegemony operating in real time rather than in the archival past: UAH's newsroom can publish "the documents" as an authoritative timeline precisely because it holds the documents, while the family's account of a forged signature and a larger stolen acreage has no equivalent institutional platform. It is also a live instance of what W. E. B. Du Bois called a "Propaganda of History" — not necessarily a fabrication, but a cleaner, institution-favoring account built from the records the institution chose to keep and publish.

Gaps and cautions

  • The forgery allegation is unresolved. No handwriting analysis, court finding, or independent document examination of the 1958 deed signature has been located. The claim that Willie Jones signed with an "X" elsewhere, and could not have signed this deed, comes from family testimony reported by WHNT News 19 — a strong lead, not a proven fact.
  • The acreage discrepancy (2.25 acres vs. 10–12 acres) is unresolved. Both figures come from named, citable sources, but we have not independently verified either against the original survey plats.
  • We have not located the complete original condemnation and payment file — only the excerpted timeline UAH has published and the family's public statements. A full independent review, which the family and Where Is My Land have publicly requested, has not yet occurred as far as this project can document.
  • No documented genealogical link has been established between Willie and Lola Jones and the Avalon-era Jones family (Lewellen Jones, Alexander P. Jones, Columbus Jones, Shandy Wesley Jones). Any shared surname is not, by itself, evidence of a family connection — this remains an open and testable question, not a claim made anywhere on this site.
  • The G. W. Jones and Sons engineering sheets are undated beyond an approximate mid-1960s estimate based on the correspondence they were filed with; an archival date range should be confirmed with the repository before this evidence is treated as more than circumstantial.
  • The Oct. 22, 1792 marriage entry rests on register OCR and indexer reads. The image tiles for DGS 007578998, image 61, failed to render, so the handwriting has not been visually verified. The marriage is confirmed from the primary record; the visual read is still owed.
  • Full analysis is pending for the newly documented institutional records — the 1971 Swartz Property deed memo, the 1966–67 UAH easement correspondence, and the 1866–68 Huntsville deed records. Their existence is documented in the project archive; the interpretation above is provisional until the complete files are read.
