Institutional Philanthropy

Plantation wealth did not stay on the plantation. This page follows the thesis's account of how money made from human bondage was converted into churches, colleges, railroads, and civic institutions — and what that conversion hides.

How the thesis frames this. The paper's literature review treats philanthropy as one of the mechanisms of institutional erasure — under the heading “INSTITUTIONAL PHILANTHROPY AND THE LEGITIMATION OF PLANTATION WEALTH.” Assets produced through slavery acquired the public legitimacy of philanthropy and improvement, while the people whose labor produced them disappeared from the story the institution tells about its own founding.

When slavery's profits became respectable

Alabama slaveholders converted plantation wealth into religious, educational, transportation, and civic institutions. The thesis cites James B. Sellers's documentation: Jeremiah H. Brown's endowment of the Brown Chair of Theology at Howard College (now Samford University), and Abner McGehee's investment in railroad stock and benefactions to the Alabama Bible Society.

Money made from human bondage does not arrive at a seminary or a rail line with a label attached; it simply becomes a chair, a stock certificate, a line in a ledger of good works — while the people whose labor produced it disappear from the story the institution tells about its own founding.

Capital conversion: philanthropy as alibi

The thesis borrows Pierre Bourdieu's account of capital conversion to name the mechanism: economic capital accumulated through slavery was converted into symbolic capital — the durable legitimacy of a named chair, a Bible society benefaction, a railroad investment — while the origins of that wealth were laundered out of the institution's story about itself.

Philanthropy, in this frame, is not the opposite of extraction but its alibi: the moment at which tainted wealth is transmuted into honor, and the people whose labor produced it disappear from the ledger of good works.

What this doesn't claim: these examples do not mean that every Alabama church, college, railroad, or charitable organization was funded directly by slavery — and they do not establish a direct financial connection between those specific benefactions and the later UAH campus. What they demonstrate is the broader process: wealth accumulated in a slave economy could be converted into durable institutional authority while the enslaved people whose labor generated it stayed absent from institutional origin stories.

The local documentary route: the Donnell papers

The finding aid for the James Webb Smith Donnell Papers offers a concrete archival basis for studying this process up close. It identifies the Donnell and John Nelson Spotswood Jones families as large landowners and prosperous planters — and describes records concerning the purchase of enslaved people, land transactions, taxes, the Memphis and Charleston Railroad, and postwar contracts with freedpeople. Plantation property, transportation finance, family networks, and labor relations intersect in a single collection.

The evidence presently supports a documented connection between John Nelson Spotswood Jones, his daughter Maria Louisa Jones Donnell, James Webb Smith Donnell, and the plantation known as Seclusion near Jonesboro in Lawrence County. Druid Grove is where Maria Louisa Jones married Donnell in 1839.

Fortune's marker

A named enslaved man, Fortune, died at Seclusion in February 1859 — and was afterward memorialized with an inscribed granite grave marker erected by Donnell himself. It is one of the very few individually marked graves of an enslaved person identified anywhere in this project's records to date.

A single stone, cut and inscribed at an enslaver's order, does more to individuate one man's death than the entire probate apparatus does for the thousands of people it otherwise records only as line items.

Separate histories stay separate: the evidence does not support treating Seclusion, Druid Grove, Quietdale in northeast Huntsville, the Madison County Jones plantation, and Robert A. Jones's North Carolina properties as one continuous plantation enterprise or one Black community. A shared surname, geographic proximity, or later local tradition does not by itself establish the connection. This study insists on documentary chains — deeds, probate records, correspondence, business accounts, migration records, documented kinship — precisely so it doesn't reproduce the white genealogical assumption that a shared surname is a shared history.
