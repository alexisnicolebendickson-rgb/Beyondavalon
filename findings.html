Recent Findings

This project moves fast. New findings land here first — dated, sourced, and labeled with their verification status — before they're worked into the deeper pages. Newest first.

How to read the labels. Documented = stated in a primary or institutional source; Under investigation = being verified; Unverified = cited with corroboration still pending. Nothing here is final — findings are corrected in place when the evidence changes, and corrections are noted, not silently made.
September 24, 2026

TVAR's social record is empty: no trace of the 2022 survey — and the roster checks out Documented

A September 2026 check of TVAR's (Tennessee Valley Archaeological Research) LinkedIn company page and public Facebook presence found zero posts about the May 2022 GPR survey and no mention of a mandible anywhere in the checked content — the firm's own channels carry no trace of the work, consistent with the May 2022 UAH article being the only public record. Personnel verified on LinkedIn: Hunter Johnson (President), Kate Manning (Principal Investigator and Field Coordinator), Travis Rael (Laboratory Director), and Ashley Stewart (Bioarchaeologist). The named surveyor, Claiborne Sea, is not listed in TVAR's company roster — his independent profile lists him as a University of Alabama PhD candidate, formerly a geophysical/GIS consultant and now with ArchaeoSpatial Analytics. The survey's only other public trace: a May 17, 2022 post in the Jackson County Historical Association Facebook group — "UAH is doing its best to locate grave sites in a grid analysis" — 54 likes, the day after UAH's announcement. Facebook's timeline is login-gated, so this is a public-portion negative, not an exhaustive search. Full sequence: What UAH Knew: Burial Timeline.

September 24, 2026

Media recovered: the WAFF 2022 story is real, and the ~60 figure traces to it Documented

Stefante Randall's February 21, 2022 WAFF 48 Black History Month story on unmarked slave graves at UAH has been located and read in full (it was previously listed as Unverified). Two things matter: it reported a separate 2017 GPR survey between Roberts Hall, the Nursing School, and the Jones Perkins Family Cemetery — aimed at detecting enslaved housing, with finds of animal bones, glass bricks, and a horseshoe from stable keeping — distinct from the May 2022 TVAR scan; and it is the earliest located source for the "about 60 graves" figure just south of campus on Ben Graves Drive, pinning that number's public circulation to media coverage, not a published UAH survey. It also records a history-department committee to erect monuments and a research fund, plus student Emily Therrell's verdict: "It's not that UAH tries to hide it, but they don't acknowledge it." Figure conflicts flagged as circulation, not resolution: the story repeats "20 slaves at purchase" (against the manuscript's 47) and "20 to 121 by the Civil War" (against the manuscript's 96).

Glenwood context joins the record as the local precedent for the GPR work: Kelly Kazek's July 28, 2015 AL.com piece and its July 29, 2015 Huntsville Times companion are cited (title, byline, and date only), and TVAR's June 2021 Glenwood GPR survey — 27 unmarked graves identified — confirms the firm UAH hired was the established local outfit for exactly this work. Full sequence: What UAH Knew: Burial Timeline.

September 24, 2026

Evidence sweep: the surveyor is real, the 2019 text is recovered, the cemetery has a second neighbor Documented Under investigation

"Claiborne Sea" — the name on the 2022 GPR survey — is a real person: a University of Alabama archaeology doctoral student and GPR specialist. TVAR was the established local firm for exactly this work (Glenwood Cemetery, 2021: 27 unmarked graves identified), yet the May 2022 UAH announcement remains the only public record of the campus survey — absent even from TVAR's own project pages. The deleted 2019 monument announcement's full text was recovered from search-engine cache, including Adan's line that "Many members of the UAH community are unaware that one of the largest plantations in Madison County was right here on campus" — no genuine Wayback capture exists yet.

The campus cemetery's paper trail also grew: Find a Grave lists "Perkins Cemetery in Huntsville" (ID 25349; 34.73220, −86.64250, published claim); an independent 2012/2014 survey transcribed all four standing markers and documented a second campus cemetery, the "Jones (Mary) Cemetery" near the daycare playground; and the cemetery is not on the Alabama Historical Commission's Historic Cemetery Register, with no public state site number located. Full sequence: What UAH Knew: Burial Timeline.

September 24, 2026

The capital mechanism: slavery-and-capitalism scholarship joins the site Documented

Three registers of the same claim now frame the site's wealth argument on the Institutional Philanthropy page: Julia Ott's mechanism (enslaved people as "self-augmenting capital"; slave-backed bonds through Baring Brothers, Brown Brothers, and the Rothschilds; $2 billion in enslaved wealth — "a third of the wealth owned by all U.S. citizens"); Beckert and Rockman's Slavery's Capitalism (raw cotton as more than half of U.S. exports; slavery's "second flourishing"); and Matthew Desmond's 1619 Project essay ("In order to understand the brutality of American capitalism, you have to start on the plantation"; "Cotton slavery was too big to fail"). The site's own records show the mechanism at work locally: Dearing v. Lightfoot (1844) has Hercules and Solomon mortgaged at $2,000 each in 1840.

September 24, 2026

Post-emancipation context: tenant farming, Black schools, the sister campus, cooperatives Documented Proposal

Dwayne Cox's history of Alabama agriculture (tenant farming replaced slavery as the state's primary labor source; the furnishing-merchant squeeze; roughly equal Black and white tenant farmers by 1920) now frames the tenant-lien discussion on the Debt & Labor page — including Reuben Jones's February 1872 crop lien. Alabama A&M's official history corroborates William Hooper Councill's 1869 Lincoln Normal School on the history timeline, alongside new Reconstruction-era HBCU context (Alabama State 1867, Talladega 1869, Tuskegee 1881, Oakwood 1896). The sister-campus sidebar gains Mozingo's specifics (Lawrence's 1831 sale to the trustees, 30,750 shingles in July 1846, Benagh's $7,000 trip). And the restorative-justice proposals add a sixth item — cooperative economics, drawn from Nonprofit Quarterly — flagged as present-day framing, not evidence.

September 24, 2026

Deed indexes: freedpeople buying land out of the Jones network's own estates Documented Under investigation

The overnight Madison County Probate deed-index sweep (official portal, signed-in session) turned up the project's strongest land lead yet: the Alexander P. Jones estate, "by Reg[ister]," conveyed land in 1870 to Prince Bradford (Deed Book OO, p. 568) and Johnson Beasley (OO, p. 596) — the same two men who bought 1870 land through the Donnell estate's Register's deeds (OO 556/559). Two estates, one year, the same two buyers: a freedmen land-purchase channel. Race is not stated in the indexes, so "freedpeople buyer" is a candidate status until the deed images are read. The Bradford cluster deepens: "Evilena Lee Jones et al." conveyed to Prince Bradford in 1877 (BBB 389) — "Evilena" is very likely the indexer's spelling of Eveline, wife of Lee Jones and sister of Columbus Jones (Freedman's Bank account 29): a Jones-descendant grantor selling to the Bradford buyer, a strong lead, not a merge. And the sweep's only index-marked Black buyer: "Phil Eldridge (Col)" bought from Jas. I. and Minnie P. Jones in 1879 (DDD 78).

The catch: the lettered deed books (A–RR) are not viewable online — the portal's non-indexed books list only numbered volumes 442–770, and index VIEW links open index pages, not deeds. Deed-level reading (race, prices, parcels, any Avalon or enslaved-person mentions) needs the Probate Judge's office — 256-532-3330 — or an in-person visit. Priority pull list: OO 556/559/568/594/596, BBB 389, DDD 78, CCC 462, PP 562, QQ 30. Full record: paper notes [489]–[492]; DB1 rows 1738–1754.

September 24, 2026

Rathbun Freedman's Bank, Vol. 1 — folded in Documented

The full extraction of Fred C. Rathbun's Names from Huntsville, Alabama, 1865–1869 (NARA M816, Roll 1, accounts 1–385) is now in the research databases and paper notes. Columbus Jones's Account No. 29 (opened October 2, 1867) names his father Turner Moore — died March 1867 at Columbus's home — and his mother Eliza Jones — died fall 1861 at her son's home — alongside his spouse Tomietta Jones and his brothers Reuben and Turner, both living in 1867.

Also confirmed: Lucion and William Jones are brothers, through parents Rolley and Gilly/Gilley Jones. And a clean negative — no Perkins appears anywhere in accounts 1–385, which constrains where the Perkins line can be found in the bank records.

September 24, 2026

Limestone County: Reuben Jones's marriage isn't there — but his crop lien is Documented

The entire unindexed tail of the Limestone County 1869–1874 Colored marriage book (book pages 117–280) was scanned page by page: no Reuben Jones appears as a groom anywhere in the volume. The Master Index citation "JONES, REUBEN — p. 126" is wrong — book page 126 is William Collin and Winnie Blackwell, married January 15, 1871. The Limestone marriage-book route is closed; the search moves to Madison County or an unrecorded marriage.

New positive find in the same sweep: Reuben Jones granted a crop/rent lien to R. B. Patton Jr., Limestone County Deed Book 14, page 368, around February 1872 — placing Reuben farming in Limestone County in early 1872 in a tenant or furnishing relationship. (A separate sheriff's sale deed on the same pages, sheriff to William G. Lewis for $100, does not involve Reuben.)

September 24, 2026

Two 1958 letters warned UAH's predecessor about the graveyard in the construction footprint Documented

Kathleen Paul Jones, a genealogist, wrote to Dr. Philip M. Mason of the University Center (the institution that became UAH) on June 1, 1958, after her son showed her the model layout of the planned campus: "When our son, Harvie Jones, showed me the model lay-out of the University Center, I asked 'What are you going to do with Lewellen Jones?'" — asking what would become of the Lewellen Jones graveyard in the construction footprint. A June 8 follow-up thanks Mason — "it is good to know the old graveyard will be cared for" — and warns other Revolutionary soldiers' resting places are "about to be engulfed by progress." Source: Frances Cabaniss Roberts Collection, Ser. 4, Subser. B, Box 1, Folder 12, UAH Archives.

See the full dated sequence on the What UAH Knew: Burial Timeline page.

September 24, 2026

Evidence sweep: what UAH's own record shows it knew Documented Under investigation

A sweep of UAH's own publications, board minutes, the student press, and local news established a dated institutional record: UAH Archives photographed the "Jones family cemetery" behind Morton Hall in 1988; UAH announced a two-sided cemetery monument in 2019 — the announcement page is now deleted (404) and no completion was ever announced; UAH hired Tennessee Valley Archaeological Research for a three-day GPR scan in May 2022 "in an attempt to locate where enslaved people were laid to rest" — and never published the results.

Honest negatives from the same sweep: no cemetery content in the checked Board of Trustees minutes (most 2018–2022 meetings remain unchecked — a coverage gap, not proof), and no press record of any UAH construction project encountering human remains. Full timeline: What UAH Knew: Burial Timeline.
