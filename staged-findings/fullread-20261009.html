A Civil War memoir in her own hand: Jennie Jones Glass Documented

An 11-page handwritten memoir, now fully transcribed, written by Jennie Jones Glass — the third daughter of Dr. B. Rush Jones of Montgomery, the same man who bought a thirteen-year-old boy called Bob at a Mobile courthouse auction in 1853 for $665. She watched Jefferson Davis's inauguration from the Capitol gallery. She watched Wilson's Raiders burn a thousand bales of cotton. And she wrote down what happened to four young Black men the Yankees took: Henry, Dennis, and Buck were carried to Cuba and sold in 1873 — eight years after emancipation — during the yellow-fever epidemic. A fourth, Milus, stayed on as butler. She also wrote a first-person account of Klansmen in white robes burning sulphur as they raided a mass meeting at the colored church near Oakwood Cemetery. This is what the archive looks like when an enslaver's daughter writes it down: the trafficking, the terror, and the names — in her own hand. Full record: DB1; paper note [1019].

Anthony Jones could sign his name — and the government paid him Documented

Anthony Jones, formerly enslaved by Alexander Jones of Madison County, filed Southern Claims Commission Claim No. 3137 on June 27, 1871. He claimed $225. The Commission allowed $125. He was literate — he signed his own name to the filing, and a white witness corroborated that Jones had been permitted to own property as early as 1858, while still enslaved. Set that against the Commission's broader pattern: Nancy Jones got 17.5 cents on the dollar for a corn crop the Commission presumed an enslaved woman couldn't have grown; Rachael Jones, widow of a U.S. Colored Troops soldier, got nothing at all. Anthony Jones's file is a first-person federal record of a Jones-enslaved claimant who could write — and who made the government pay. Full record: DB1; paper note [1020].

106 people: the 1860 Jones slaveholders, pinned Documented

The 1860 Madison County slave schedules name exactly three Jones holders of thirty or more enslaved people: Alex. P. Jones, 106 — the eighth-largest holder in the county; G. F. Jones, 46; G. W. Jones, 49. A separate schedule read this week adds "Abr. P. Jones" with about twenty more. One hundred and six human beings on one man's schedule, in a county where the 1860 census counted 14,573 enslaved people against 11,685 white residents. The number is not an estimate or a secondary account — it is the federal schedule, page 483, Northwestern Division. Full record: DB1; paper note [1021].

October 9, 2026

Nine free Black slaveholders in 1830 Madison County Documented

Carter G. Woodson's Free Negro Owners of Slaves in the United States in 1830 tabulates nine free Black slaveholders in Madison County: Betsey Davis, James F. Stewart, John Robinson, Paschal Blanks, Lewis Hunt, Nancy Hunster, Jenny Findley, John Evans, and Andrew Winn. Free Black people holding enslaved people — in the same county, in the same decade, where the Jones plantation was built on enslaved labor. The archive does not sort neatly into victim and villain; it sorts into people navigating a system designed to crush them, making whatever choices survival allowed. Their names are published here because they were people, not because their choices were simple. Full record: DB1; paper note [1022].

Five years after emancipation: Black Jones households holding land Documented

The 1870 census, verified from the manuscript page, shows four adjacent Black Jones-headed households in Huntsville — dwellings 84 through 87: Jerry Jones, 56, farmer, $800 in personal estate; Phil Jones, 23, farmer, $150 in real estate plus $300 personal; Lee Jones, 46, farmer, $800 personal — all born in slavery, all illiterate on the census taker's page, all holding property five years after emancipation. This is the project's standing question made concrete: freedpeople buying and holding land and capital on or near the plantation landscape. The next step is mapping these households against the Avalon plat. Full record: DB1; DB4; paper note [1023].
