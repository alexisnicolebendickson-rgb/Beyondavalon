Slave & Convict Leasing

This page documents hiring-out practices, forced family separation through sale, and Alabama's post-emancipation convict-leasing system as traced in the Avalon/Jones research network. It was moved here from the interactive Plantations Map so this material is read deliberately rather than encountered inside map markers.

Why this page exists. This material was originally presented inside the interactive Plantations Map's "Bondage & Labor Systems" panel. It has been moved to its own page so it can be read deliberately, cited fully, and linked to directly — rather than surfacing unexpectedly inside map markers. No text, citation, or sourcing has been altered or removed in the move.

Slave leasing, hiring out, & convict leasing

These are the specific, documented mechanisms of coercion this research has traced so far — not a general history of slavery, but what the Avalon/Jones-network evidence base currently shows. We do not soften this material: enslaved people were legally property, divided among heirs like furniture, and that violence continued in new forms after 1865.

Hiring out & labor contracts

  • 2 Aug. 1865, Calhoun County: A Freedmen's Bureau labor contract between B. F. Teague and a freedwoman named Caroline was witnessed by A. C. Jones — within months of emancipation, a member of this family network was already positioned inside the new labor-contract system that replaced explicit ownership with continued economic control over Black labor. Evidence database, row 410.
  • Hiring out enslaved people for wages paid to their enslaver — rather than to the enslaved person doing the work — was common practice across the antebellum South, including in urban Huntsville. We have not yet located a hiring-out contract specifically naming an Avalon-network enslaver; this is a targeted next research step (Madison County Orphans Court and probate hiring-out records, 1820-1865).

    • Convict leasing after emancipation

  • After 1865, Alabama built a state and county convict-leasing system that targeted Black men — often convicted under vagrancy, "false pretense," or petty-theft statutes written to criminalize freedom itself — and leased them to coal mines, furnaces, turpentine camps, and farms, frequently under worse conditions than slavery because the lessee had no long-term stake in the leased person's survival. Secondary source: Douglas A. Blackmon, Slavery by Another Name: The Re-Enslavement of Black Americans from the Civil War to World War II (New York: Doubleday, 2008); logged in the evidence database, rows 420-423.
  • Elyton/Jones Valley and Arlington/The Grove (Jefferson County, on the Plantations Map as Tier 2 sites) sit in the historic heart of Alabama's convict-leasing economy — the Birmingham-district mines and furnaces that were the system's largest customers. We have not yet found a primary source connecting the Avalon/Jones family directly to convict leasing. This is flagged as an open research question, not a claim: next steps are the Madison County Commissioners' Court minutes (1866-1901), Alabama Department of Corrections lease reports, and the Alabama Department of Archives and History's convict-department records.

    • Slave trading & forced family separation

  • 1820, Madison County: On Lewellen Jones's death, his estate — including 40 enslaved people — was divided between his two sons; Alexander Jones inherited Avalon plantation and 20 of the 40. A division "by estate" like this routinely separated enslaved families across two households with no say in the matter. Source: "2020 and the Future of Alabama History: The Avalon Plantation Marker Story" panel transcript, logged in the evidence database, row 413.
  • 1848, Petersburg: A deed names Alexander Jones and Robert Atkinson alongside two enslaved men recorded only as "Charles and James" — a transfer of human beings reduced in the document to first names with no surname, the standard erasure of identity in a bill of sale. Evidence database, row 411.
  • 1866, Madison County: The J. Haywood Jones estate sale notice lists enslaved people among the property to be liquidated at the dissolution of an estate in this same family network. See the Documents section of the home page.
  • On Lewellen Jones's 1820 death, an enslaved woman named Eliza Jones and her three biracial children were manumitted — the exception that proves the rule: manumission required a white man's decision, not the enslaved person's freedom to claim it. Researchers speculate a Jones family member fathered these children; this is flagged in our records as speculation, not documented fact. Evidence database, row 413.

    • This page will grow as evidence is confirmed. Distinguish carefully between what a document states, what a document implies, and what remains an open question — each entry above is labeled accordingly. If you have documents, family knowledge, or leads on any of these mechanisms, please get in touch.
