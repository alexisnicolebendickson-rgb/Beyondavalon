From the thesis · Chapter 2: The literature review

Archival Violence and Mnemonic Hegemony

The documents that preserve Black life are often the same instruments that violated it. This page follows the thesis chapter on how the archive was built to organize Black people as property — and how that design still decides what Huntsville is allowed to remember.

How to read this page. Claims are marked at their evidence level: Documented = stated in a primary or scholarly source; Under investigation = the record is being verified; Disputed = sources conflict. Where the paper itself flags a claim as needing a better citation, this page says so rather than hiding it.

The finding aid as an instrument

Epistemic violence in this archive was not incidental; it was underwritten by deliberate archival strategy. Scholarship on the federal Slave Schedules has shown how that data collection was shaped to support paternalistic defenses of slavery — and twentieth-century archival categorization compounded the problem by consolidating Black history into generalized folders organized around white surnames. Documented.

The Jones family papers, 1784–1940, illustrate the pattern at the collection level: the collection is organized around the white Jones lineage, and its temporal and geographic scope suggests it may also document enslaved and later free Black Jones households whose identities remain submerged under the family surname that enslaved them. Until such collections are re-indexed to foreground Black actors, their archival existence contributes little to public understanding of Black kinship and labor histories. Under investigation.

This project answers with a counter-archive: a structured evidence catalog that logs each Jones-related probate, census, chancery, and claims record under Black and mixed-heritage lineages rather than white surnames alone — a micro-scale prototype for the reparative re-indexing archival scholars have called for. The discipline here follows Saidiya Hartman's warning: the historian risks reproducing the archive's violence in the act of recovery. So this project refuses to force unresolved kinships into a competing narrative. It names the silences as silences, so that absence itself becomes evidence of how the record was made.

The schedule that counts — and the marshal's tally

The 1850 Slave Schedule lists 139 enslaved people under Alexander P. Jones in Madison County; the 1860 manuscript, read entry by entry, gives 96 — not the 106 that UAH's 2019 announcement repeated. The published 106 is superseded. Documented.

A recent scholarly study reported 25 of the 1850 enslaved as “fugitives from the state,” a figure that complicated paternalistic accounts of Jones as an unusually permissive enslaver. A direct line-by-line verification of the microfilm does not sustain that count: a single explicit fugitive mark survives, and the roughly twenty check-like marks in the fugitive column are the enumerator's tally convention — they track mulatto entries across neighboring pages, including on infants and toddlers, and cannot be read as flight. The higher figure, which persists in the study's own published footnote, rests on a misreading of the marshal's bookkeeping, not on the schedule itself. Documented — a correction to published scholarship.

Even a document built to erase a person's name from the citizenry can, in the same stroke, be the only place that person's name survives: while federal census schedules omitted names, local chancery and probate records preserved them as line items in estate settlements.

Erasure with a destination

Institutional erasure also worked by physically removing people from the American record altogether. One source documents the 1873 trafficking of three men — Buck, Dennis, and Henry — who were taken to Cuba and sold, while a fourth, Milus, died in Havana. This is not archival neglect; it is a form of erasure with a destination — men deleted from the domestic legal record precisely so they could be re-commodified in a foreign jurisdiction where no American court could ever be asked to account for them. Documented.

The 1866 Freedmen's Bureau annual report identifies the “apprenticeship system” as a legal tool by which county officials and probate judges could forcibly bind orphaned Black children as laborers — coercion conducted through the polite language of the court docket. Documented.

That continuity did not end with Reconstruction. Dora Jones, born in Athens, Alabama, was held in conditions of involuntary servitude for approximately forty years; her release was not secured until 1946, four years after Congress's own formal anti-peonage legislation had supposedly closed that door. Her case, geographically proximate to the Avalon network, demonstrates that the carceral mechanisms the 1866 Freedmen's Bureau report describes were not relics of Reconstruction. They were operational instruments, sustained into living memory. Documented.

The courtroom as clearinghouse

Alabama law codified a system in which courts assessed a monetary value for enslaved individuals — Jack at $600 (roughly $20,000 in today's purchasing power), Reuben at $850 (roughly $29,000) — and compensated their owners for half that assessment upon the individual's execution. A court, sentencing a man to death, simultaneously set a price for what his death would cost his owner: a judiciary functioning as a clearinghouse for human property, treating execution not as a state-sanctioned death but as a reimbursable business loss. Documented.

The name that isn't in the record

The July 2026 Hoksbergen and DeNeefe study establishes that the designation “Avalon” carries no primary-source authority — no known antebellum document refers to the plantation by that name. Its modern currency is a convention of secondary and popular literature: an institutional name applied retrospectively, severing the site from its documented legal and genealogical history. That is itself an instance of the archival violence this page describes. Documented.

The same study's prosopography of free people of color shows how even enumerated Black households were fragmented through race-coding and inconsistent naming — Tuscaloosa County lists that include Shandy Jones and his relatives classify them through single-letter complexion codes rather than humanizing descriptors: a family reduced to a letter grade on a census taker's page.

The Black press as counter-archive

Huntsville's white-owned Democrat and Mercury are heavily cited in municipal histories, while the Huntsville Gazette — a Black newspaper with surviving issues from the 1880s and 1890s — remains largely unexamined in local scholarship. Preliminary inventory of Gazette clippings in this project's evidence corpus suggests it reported directly on Black political life, labor, and church activity, yet these accounts have not been integrated into the canonical narrative of Huntsville's development. The absence of systematic scholarly engagement is not a gap. It is a choice, repeated by omission for over a century. Under investigation.

The state worked to make sure no Black counter-narrative could be written at all: the 1832 anti-literacy provision barred teaching “a slave or free person of color to spell, read, or write” on pain of a $250–$500 fine, and the 1901 Alabama Constitution codified white supremacy into governance through literacy tests that wrote Black residents out of the body politic. In Tuscaloosa County in 1884, a committee of Black men petitioned for inclusion on jury lists under Alabama Code Section 4733, only to be told that jury-list “competence” was a matter of each official's own judgment — a facially neutral standard applied, in practice, to exclude. Documented.

Why this page exists. Mnemonic hegemony is not only about what tours and markers say. It lives in the infrastructural regimes that silently organize what can be remembered: probate indices, chancery registers, archival finding aids, campus master plans, condemnation dockets. This project reads UAH's Morton Hall graves, the Perkins-Jones cemetery, and the siting of academic buildings alongside those record systems — because the landscape itself is a document, and it has been edited.
