From the thesis

Restorative Justice and Memorialization Gaps

Scholarship on reparations and institutional accountability outlines a spectrum of responses to histories of slavery and dispossession — but those frameworks have not been systematically applied to Huntsville or the Avalon/UAH lands. This page names what peer institutions have actually done, what repair could concretely look like here, and the evidentiary work this project does first.

The argument

Scholarship on reparations and institutional accountability outlines a spectrum of responses to histories of slavery and dispossession — public apologies, memorials, endowed scholarships for descendants, community benefit agreements, land-return initiatives, and formal partnerships with affected families. Yet these frameworks have not been systematically applied to Huntsville or to the Avalon/UAH lands, and the literature on universities' memory projects warns that acknowledgment alone rarely alters ownership structures or redirects capital flows. Documented (as scholarship).

UAH's institutional publications in 2019 and 2022 announced a historical marker project for the Avalon/Jones-Perkins cemetery and highlighted ongoing archaeological investigations — yet no publicly available records document whether the marker was installed, dedicated, or deferred. That is precisely the gap between institutional recognition and concrete restorative action the literature predicts. Documented.

This project does not prescribe remedies. It does the work the thesis argues must come first: reconstructing the legal and genealogical chain — from the 1825 slave-sale revenues through antebellum trust deeds, the Southern Claims Commission files, the tenant-lien systems, and the mid-twentieth-century condemnation of the Willie Jones homestead — assembling the evidentiary basis necessary for any future claims about land, lineage, and institutional responsibility. The move is from a general call for “recognition” toward specific, documentable claims. The chain is traced across this site: the wealth investigation, the claims files, the debt machinery, and the condemnation.

Two gaps the thesis names explicitly: there is still no systematic analysis of Huntsville Gazette coverage or other Black-run press that might reframe Reconstruction-era and early Jim Crow history from Black readers' and writers' perspective — and further research is still needed to link the extracted labor this site documents to the long-term accumulation of capital in the region's educational and industrial infrastructure. Under investigation.

What other institutions have actually done Documented precedents

The national landscape is not hypothetical — peer institutions have put money and structure behind acknowledgment. In April 2019, Georgetown undergraduates voted for a nonbinding $27.20-per-semester “Reconciliation Contribution” to benefit descendant communities of the 272 enslaved people the university sold in 1838; the president countered with a $400,000 donor-funded initiative. After conducting historical audits, Virginia Theological Seminary created a $1.7 million endowment and Princeton Theological Seminary created a $27.6 million endowment tied to their slavery histories. Roughly 56 universities have joined the Universities Studying Slavery consortium (led by the University of Virginia). Source: Associated Press, December 2019. Documented (as reported).

The in-system precedent is closer to home: in 2004, the University of Alabama faculty passed a resolution formally acknowledging and apologizing for the flagship's history with slavery — documented across thirty-seven years of enslaved campus labor in Ford Mozingo's “The Changing Nature of Slavery at the University of Alabama, 1828–1865” (Crimson Historical Review; PDF). Documented.

The field's working framework comes from Ithaka S+R's survey of institutional slavery-history projects, which found three things in common across universities: many colleges would not have been founded without slavery; the support of slavery was often a founding vision and influence; and institutions kept reaping benefits from enslaved labor well beyond their founding. That is the lens this project holds up to UAH. Documented (as scholarship).

What repair could concretely look like at UAH Proposals — not commitments

These are specific, checkable proposals drawn from the precedents above — things a future administration, board, or community coalition could do and be measured against. None of them have been adopted; all are Proposals.

  • Publish the GPR results. Release the full Tennessee Valley Archaeological Research reports from the May 2022 survey — methods, anomaly maps, interpretations — the way a research university publishes research. Proposal
  • Complete the cemetery monument and footpath. Finish what the 2019 announcement promised: the two-sided Jones-Perkins Family Cemetery marker on the footpath between Morton Hall and Union Grove Gallery, with the enslaved burials named as such. Proposal
  • Fund descendant-community research. Endow or annually fund independent genealogical and archaeological research controlled in part by descendant communities — the Georgetown and seminary endowments are the documented models. Proposal
  • Join Universities Studying Slavery. Put UAH's records — the 1958 letters, the 1988 photograph, the 2019 and 2022 announcements — into the consortium's shared evidentiary commons. Proposal
  • Commission the independent audit. A Princeton-style historical audit of the campus land's full chain — plantation, tenancy, condemnation, construction — with findings published regardless of what they show. Proposal
  • Redirect the capital flow: cooperative economics. A Nonprofit Quarterly analysis argues the plantation's extractive logic persists in modern Alabama through low-wage manufacturing and the prison system — Alabama Correctional Industries reported $13.66 million in 2023 revenue and $3.15 million in profit while incarcerated workers earned $0.15–$0.25 an hour — and points to worker cooperatives, including the Federation of Southern Cooperatives' 70-plus co-ops serving 20,000 families, as the way out. A UAH-backed commitment to Black-led cooperative enterprise in Huntsville would aim beyond recognition at the capital flow itself. Present-day framing, not evidence. Proposal

The dated evidence behind these proposals is laid out on What UAH Knew: A Burial Timeline; the national context on Recent Findings as new material lands.
