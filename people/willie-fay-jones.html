Willie (Fay) Jones

The man who lost the well. In 1954 the City of Huntsville condemned the well site on his family’s Athens Pike land; in 1958 he sold the parcel. His siblings carry the land-reclamation fight today — Black ownership taken and destroyed, in a single life.

DRAFT — not for publication. Staged for review September 2026. Nothing on this page goes live without the researcher’s word. Evidence levels: Documented Under investigation Unresolved
How to read this page. Every claim names its source. Where the record is silent, this page says so instead of filling the gap. This page adds to the researcher’s thesis — that in Madison County, Black-owned land was taken and destroyed while white generational land was leased and kept — and it does not take that thesis over. Life in brief

Willie (Fay) Jones was a Black landowner on Athens Pike in Huntsville, Alabama — the family’s 2.25-acre parcel sitting about three miles northwest of the city, with the family well on it. Documented In 1954, the City of Huntsville condemned a sixty-by-forty-foot site around that well, in Madison County Probate Cause No. 12257. The December 30, 1954 decree set the award at $900; on appeal it was raised to $1,250. Documented On October 15, 1958, Willie Jones and his wife Lola Mae Jones sold two tracts — including the 2.25-acre parcel — to W. L. Sanderson Realty, recorded in Deed Book 265, page 314. Documented

The parcel’s paper trail runs on: the City of Huntsville’s 1963 annual report lists “Land Acquisition Office — Chief—W. L. Sanderson” — a documented position, stated here as fact and nothing more. Documented In 1963–64 the land passed through Equity Cause No. 18604, in which the University of Alabama Board of Trustees appeared with a one-eighth interest. Documented And the story is not over: from 2023 to 2025, five surviving Jones siblings carried the land-reclamation fight into public view — a partnership with Where Is My Land, a September 18, 2023 press release, news investigations, and City Council follow-up. Documented

Whether Willie was the great-grandson of Reuben Jones — the Reconstruction legislator — is the project’s staged hypothesis, not a finding. Deed Book 265, page 314 is the test: the deed that would connect the two names. Until it is read, the kinship is not stated as fact on this page. Under investigation

Records

1954–1956

The condemnation — Probate Cause No. 12257 Documented

The City of Huntsville condemns the sixty-by-forty-foot well site on the Jones family’s 2.25-acre Athens Pike parcel. Decree December 30, 1954: $900. Raised to $1,250 on appeal.

October 15, 1958

The sale — Deed Book 265, page 314 Documented

Willie Jones and Lola Mae Jones sell two tracts, including the 2.25-acre parcel, to W. L. Sanderson Realty. This is the deed the great-grandson hypothesis turns on.

1963–1964

Equity Cause No. 18604 Documented

The land moves through equity litigation in which the University of Alabama Board of Trustees appears with a one-eighth interest. The City’s 1963 annual report lists W. L. Sanderson as chief of the Land Acquisition Office.

2023–2025

The reclamation fight Documented

Five surviving Jones siblings take the land-reclamation case public: the Where Is My Land partnership, the September 18, 2023 #ActivateHuntsville press release, news investigations, and City Council follow-up. Living memory, still in motion.

What the record doesn’t say

The silence ledger for Willie (Fay) Jones

  • Whether Willie was Reuben Jones’s great-grandson is untested. Deed Book 265, page 314 is the document that would settle it, and it has not yet been read firsthand.
  • Why the family sold in 1958 — four years after the condemnation cut the well out of the parcel — is not in the records this project has read. The deed records the transfer; it does not record the reason.
  • The full text of Probate Cause No. 12257 and Equity Cause No. 18604 has not yet been pulled. The case files may name every party, every award, and every acre.
  • W. L. Sanderson’s positions are documented; his motives are not. This page alleges nothing beyond what the records state.
  • Willie’s birth and death dates are not yet in the project’s records. He enters the written record as a landowner defending — and then selling — his family’s ground.
