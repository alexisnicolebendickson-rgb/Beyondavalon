Dora Jones

Born Theodora Lawrence Jones in Athens, Alabama, in 1890, the daughter of formerly enslaved people — and held in unpaid domestic labor for nearly forty years before her case broke in a California courtroom in 1947. She stands here as a Jones-network lead and a story about what enslavement’s afterlife looked like in the twentieth century.

How to read this page. Every claim names its source. Where the record is silent, this page says so instead of filling the gap. Dora’s kinship tie to the Avalon Jones network is unproven: Athens (Limestone County) is plausible geography, but Plato and Lazzie Garrett Jones appear in none of the project’s databases. She is a lead, not confirmed kin. Under investigation

Life in brief

Dora Jones was born Theodora Lawrence Jones in Athens, Alabama, in 1890, the daughter of Plato Jones — a shoemaker and brickmaker — and Lazzie Garrett Jones, both formerly enslaved. Documented As a teenager she was taken to Washington, D.C., by Elizabeth Kimball Harman Ingalls, and held there — and later in California — in unpaid domestic labor under physical abuse for nearly forty years. Documented She was discovered in Berkeley, California, in October 1946. Documented In June 1947 the federal government tried Ingalls in United States v. Ingalls in the U.S. District Court in San Diego: a sixteen-day trial ending in conviction — ten days in jail, a three-year suspended sentence, a $2,500 fine, and $6,000 in “lost wages” to Dora. Documented The case was covered by the New York Times, the Washington Post, Time, and the Sydney Morning Herald. Documented Dora Jones died March 1, 1972, in St. Louis. Documented

Why she is on this site: her parents were formerly enslaved Joneses in Athens, in the county just west of Madison — the right geography, the right name, the right generation. If a document ever connects Plato and Lazzie Garrett Jones to the Madison County network, Dora’s story becomes the twentieth-century end of the Avalon line: proof that what the plantation built did not stay in the nineteenth century. Until then, she stays where the evidence puts her — a lead, carried openly as one. Under investigation

Theodora Lawrence Jones, daughter of Plato Jones — a shoemaker and brickmaker — and Lazzie Garrett Jones, both formerly enslaved (Robert Fikes Jr., BlackPast, 2017).

c. 1905–1907

Taken to Washington, D.C. Documented

Elizabeth Kimball Harman Ingalls takes the teenage Dora to Washington, D.C., beginning nearly forty years of unpaid domestic labor under physical abuse.

October 1946

Discovered in Berkeley, California Documented

After decades in bondage, Dora is found in Berkeley, California. Her case becomes a federal prosecution.

June 1947

United States v. Ingalls Documented

Tried in the U.S. District Court, San Diego — a sixteen-day trial. Ingalls is convicted: ten days in jail, a three-year suspended sentence, a $2,500 fine, and $6,000 in “lost wages” awarded to Dora. The opinion is reported at 73 F.Supp. 761.

1947

National press coverage Documented

The trial is covered by the New York Times, the Washington Post, Time, and the Sydney Morning Herald — a Black woman’s forty years of forced labor treated, briefly, as national news.

March 1, 1972

Died in St. Louis Documented

Dora Jones dies in St. Louis, Missouri.

What the record doesn’t say

The silence ledger for Dora Jones

  • No document connects Plato Jones or Lazzie Garrett Jones to the Madison County Jones network. The kinship tie is a lead, not a finding — and it stays a lead until a document says otherwise.
  • The project’s databases contain no Plato Jones, no Lazzie Garrett Jones, and no Dora Jones of Athens — her family has not yet been traced in the census, marriage, or death records.
  • The trial record says what Ingalls did to Dora; it says almost nothing about Dora’s own life before she was taken — her childhood in Athens, her parents’ households, how Ingalls found her.
  • No record traces Dora’s life between the 1947 trial and her death in St. Louis in 1972 — twenty-five years the archive does not cover.
  • A second Dora — age forty in the 1865 Huntsville Black census, formerly enslaved by Thomas McCalley — is a different woman on a single record. She is not merged here and is not page-ready.
