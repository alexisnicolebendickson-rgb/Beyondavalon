Nancy, Marshall & Zachariah Jones

A freed family of the Alexander P. Jones plantation: Nancy’s 1876 deposition describes farming eighty acres of corn after emancipation; her husband Marshall was the plantation’s “head man”; their likely son Zachariah was denied his federal claim because slavery had denied him literacy.

DRAFT — not for publication. Staged for review September 2026. Nothing on this page goes live without the researcher’s word. Evidence levels: Documented Under investigation Unresolved
How to read this page. Every claim names its source. Where the record is silent, this page says so instead of filling the gap. Three people share this page because the Southern Claims Commission files tie them together as one family — but two facts are not settled: who enslaved Nancy (one record says Alexander P. Jones, another says “one True”), and whether Zachariah is definitively Nancy and Marshall’s son. Both conflicts are stated on this page exactly as the records give them. Under investigation

Nancy Jones — the deposition

Nancy Jones (Colored) of Madison County filed Southern Claims Commission Claim No. 2753 — the federal body that compensated Southern Unionists for property taken by the Union army — and gave her deposition on February 18, 1876. Documented In her own words, she was enslaved when the war began, was told she was free by Yankee soldiers, and after emancipation farmed some eighty acres of corn on Alex Jones’s land with her husband Marshall Jones, their children, and a plow hand. Documented She claimed $582 for her property: five hundred bushels of corn, seventeen stock hogs, three pork hogs, one milch cow, two oxen, and one black mare. The Commission allowed $102 and disallowed $480 — eighty-two and a half percent of what a Black woman said she owned, erased on the presumption that enslaved people could not have owned crops. Documented

One record names Nancy’s enslaver as Alexander P. Jones — the Madison County planter enumerated with ninety-six enslaved people in 1860. Documented Another record, in the same project database, says she was “formerly enslaved by ‘one True.’” Documented The claim file has not yet been read firsthand to resolve it — possibly two different Nancys, possibly sequential enslavers — so this page does not choose. Under investigation Her husband Marshall’s deposition, given July 8, 1876, confirms her account, and a white neighbor, John A. Hilliard, witnessed for her. Documented

Marshall Jones — the head man

Marshall Jones is described as the “head man” on Alexander P. Jones’s plantation — the management layer of the plantation, seen from the enslaved side. Documented He was about forty-six in 1870. In his deposition of July 8, 1876, given in support of Nancy’s claim, he said Alex Jones told him early in the war that he was free, and that after emancipation he farmed the “East Place” — the 1866 estate account credits him $170. Documented A named Black plantation foreman, speaking on the federal record, about the land he worked and the money it owed him. Documented

Zachariah Jones — denied for what slavery denied him

Zachariah Jones, a “colored man” of Huntsville, filed Southern Claims Commission Claim No. 2273 on June 13, 1871, seeking $365: a black mule taken in April 1864 by General Wilder’s men, three hundred pounds of beef and a bay horse taken in August 1864, and a chestnut sorrel horse taken in January or February 1865 by General Thomas’s men. Documented He received no vouchers for the property — because, denied education under slavery, he did not know that written instruments would matter. The claim was barred and disallowed: the legal system penalizing a man for the consequences of the slavery it had just abolished, in his own stated words. Documented His witnesses were Joseph Ward, Calet Jones, and A. Rice Jones. Documented

The database records him as Nancy and Marshall’s son — but one of the database’s own entries flags that kinship as an open question, and the claim file has not been read firsthand. Under investigation This page calls him their likely son and no more. He also appears as a witness in Nancy’s claim. Documented His birth year does not sit still: about twenty-five or twenty-six when he filed in 1871 puts his birth around 1845–46, while a separate 1870 hypothesis puts him at nineteen or twenty, born around 1850–51. The record does not agree with itself, and this page does not force it to. Under investigation He married Eliza Wallace on May 11, 1876, in the Madison County marriage index. Documented

What the record doesn’t say

The silence ledger for Nancy, Marshall & Zachariah Jones

  • Two records name two different enslavers for Nancy — Alexander P. Jones and “one True.” Until the claim file is read firsthand, neither is stated as fact on this page.
  • Whether Zachariah is Nancy and Marshall’s son is recorded as fact in one database entry and flagged as a question in another. The kinship stays likely, not certain.
  • Zachariah’s birth year wobbles by five years between records. No birth record names him.
  • Nancy’s full claim file — the commissioners’ summary and remarks have been read at working resolution, but the complete file may name more family, more property, and more of what was taken.
  • No record in this project yet follows this family past 1876. Where they went, and what became of the eighty acres, is the next search.
